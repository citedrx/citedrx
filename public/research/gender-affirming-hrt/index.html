Clinical Evidence · Gender-Affirming Hormone Therapy

What the guidelines and the outcomes data actually show

For adults, gender-affirming hormone therapy has an established clinical guideline (Endocrine Society), an updated standards-of-care framework that validates informed consent (WPATH SOC8), and a growing body of outcomes data specific to telehealth delivery. Here's what each actually says, in plain language.

Last reviewed: September 2026 · See the provider scores →
The short answer: the Endocrine Society's clinical practice guideline sets specific lab-monitoring targets for gender-affirming hormone therapy (feminizing: estradiol 100–200 pg/mL, testosterone under 50 ng/dL; masculinizing: testosterone 400–700 ng/dL), checked every 3 months during the first year. WPATH's 2022 Standards of Care, Version 8, explicitly validated informed consent, prescribing based on a clinician's evaluation and your understanding of risks and benefits, as a legitimate model, not a shortcut around care. A 2025 telehealth-specific cohort study of 342 adults found significant reductions in depression and anxiety within 3 months of starting therapy.

What this means in plain language

"Informed consent" is not a lesser standard: WPATH said so directly

For years, the dominant model required a letter from a mental health professional confirming readiness for treatment before a doctor could prescribe. WPATH's 2022 update explicitly recognizes informed consent, a clinician evaluating you directly and prescribing once you understand the risks and benefits, as an equally valid approach. Every provider ranked on our comparison page uses this model. It's a legitimate clinical pathway, not a corner being cut.

The mental health data is genuinely encouraging, with real caveats

The 2025 JMIR cohort study is one of the first to look specifically at telehealth-delivered hormone therapy rather than in-person specialty clinics, and found meaningful improvement in depression and anxiety scores at 3 months. That's a real, telehealth-specific finding. The honest caveat: 3 months is a short follow-up window, and the broader research base this sits within still leans on specialized-clinic samples that may not represent every patient's experience equally.

Masculinizing therapy carries a monitoring detail worth knowing: hematocrit

Testosterone therapy can raise red blood cell counts (hematocrit), a known, manageable risk that the Endocrine Society guideline specifically calls for monitoring alongside hormone levels. This isn't unique to gender-affirming testosterone: it's the same physiological effect tracked in the TRT population, and it's exactly the kind of detail our Monitoring Rigor factor is designed to surface.
